#### In class progress audit: What is your early concept? Do you have any sketches or mappings that detail the interactivity architecture? If so link to miro or other platform, or provide images of analogue working. What are the relations and storyworlds internal and external? What is left to chance and ergotics? Have you considered the visual aspect of your piece and what kind of images and design elements you will use?

My early concept is a text-based 'day in the life' simulator in which the user interacts in first-person as a character. I think of it as an anxiety simulator, with each option from launch causing a worsening or stagnation of the narrator's condition. I have hopes to make it, although with limited choices, quite expansive, allowing many different experiences, all threaded through with a Groundhog Day theme -- the user is frustratingly unable to escape the  self-deprecating, pessimistic and overly-anxious 'self'/narrator they must play as. The idea is that no matter what rabbit hole/experience they go down, all will end with the idea they can 'try again tomorrow' to have a good day, thus looping to the beginning, with no real result of being able to 'escape' this hell. The user will have options, but all of them will be limited to poor results. I have done some mapping of the first section, in which the user wakes up and chooses things to do to start the day. Their choices will lead them to either end up choosing to leave the house, or stay home. Leaving the house will lead to different sections, such as going to the store or the park, and staying home will cause them to doomscroll on their laptop. Some of the choices they can go down will mean their day ends early, with the longest path causing them to go to a party. Left to change/ergotics is their decision, and what they are tied by is that there is no satisfying finish to the game - leaning into the known frustration of lack of choice. I want to leave the user with such frustration that, strangely enough, they hate it and don't want to play, because of an assumed presumption they can somehow 'win', and the realisation that they can't.

### Thursday 14 May
From practicing Bitsy today, I'm glad I didn't use this for my piece. Only in hindsight can I see how much form changes things - the entire piece. Trying to write on Bitsy for just one screen, to tell a story, is hard enough. An entire word based piece would've changed it into something it's not. 
On that note, I've been thinking and theorising that both the code and the written aspects of a piece take so much different levels of attention an effort. I feel like the design/coding/layout/backstuff/animations and the writing/story/literature fight for a place in my piece, and only one can be stronger. I'm going between focusing on one, to then the other, then the other. I can't decide which is stronger, and I'm trying to synthesises them and my methodology for them, too, but honestly I think this battle is central to e-lit. I struggle to describe why they don't, but they just, don't. Either the writing feels like code itself, eaten by it, or the code feels lkke a background imagery to writing. 
If I chose Bitsy, the design would've won, and this story isn't one that I want to tell without words. Maybe next time.
